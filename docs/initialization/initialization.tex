\begin{abstract}
  This document describes in detail the routines within the DyCoV tool that calculate
  the steady-state initialization values for all elements of the test cases prescribed
  by the DTR PCSs (fiches)~\cite{DTRhome}. The calculation is \emph{model-agnostic}: it
  solves analytic load flows over the declared topology and the pi-model equivalents of
  its branches, so it does not depend on the type of generating unit behind each
  terminal (synchronous machine, wind turbine, PV array, or storage).
\end{abstract}





\section{Overview}

Although the producer is expected to supply his own model of the installation, he
cannot be asked to also provide the correct initialization values for all the case
scenarios prescribed by the DTR PCSs. Such initialization is dependent on many other
parameters that vary from PCS to PCS, such as the connection reactances or the values of
P, Q, V prescribed at the connection point (PDR bus). Therefore the DyCoV tool performs
its own internal calculations to find out the correct initialization values for the
generating units, the auxiliary load, the transformers, etc.; on a case-by-case basis.

The approach consists in assuming that \emph{the topology on the producer's side is
  restricted to a small set of variants}, which enables the tool to calculate the
quantities analytically, by means of relatively simple load-flow (LF) formulas expressed
in closed form. Approaches providing greater freedom remain possible in principle, such
as running the cases through one or more passes of \emph{DynaFlow}~\cite{Dynawo} until a
valid steady state is achieved, but the analytic approach covers all the topologies
admitted by the DTR test cases.

The calculation described here is implemented in the function \code{init\_calcs()}
(module \code{dycov/electrical/initialization\_calcs.py}), with the pi-model equivalent
parameters provided by \code{dycov/electrical/pimodel\_parameters.py}.


\section{Allowed topology variants}
\label{sec:topologies}

For the analytic LF approach to work, the topology on the producer's side \emph{must} be
one of these eight variants:
\begin{description}
\item[S:] single unit (synchronous machine, WT, PV, or BESS)
\item [S+i:] single unit + Line (represents an ``internal network'')
\item [S+Aux:] single unit + Aux Load
\item [S+Aux+i:] single unit + Aux Load + Line
\item [M:] multiple WT/PV/BESS units + plant transformer
\item [M+i:] multiple WT/PV/BESS units + plant transformer + Line
\item [M+Aux:] multiple WT/PV/BESS units + plant transformer + Aux Load
\item [M+Aux+i:] multiple WT/PV/BESS units + plant transformer + Aux Load + Line
\end{description}

There are here two main groups: single-unit vs.\ multiple-unit topologies. Their
composition rules, which the tool checks before running any calculation, are as follows:

\begin{itemize}
\item In the \textbf{single-unit} family the unit is connected to the rest of the
  network through its step-up transformer (\code{StepUp\_Xfmr}). Only in topology S, the
  step-up transformer may be omitted when the unit's dynamic model already includes its
  own transformer internally (models with \code{ConverterLVControl = false}); in that
  case the unit connects directly to the connection node. Synchronous machines are
  admitted only in this family.
\item In the \textbf{multiple-unit} family each unit has its own step-up transformer,
  and all of them connect to a common internal bus. This family additionally requires a
  \emph{plant-level transformer} (\code{Main\_Xfmr}) between the internal bus and the
  PDR bus (or between the internal bus and the internal line, in the ``+i'' variants).
\item In each case one may model, if desired, an ``internal network'' via a single
  \code{Line} object (the ``+i'' variants), and an auxiliary load via a single
  \code{Load} object behind its own transformer (\code{AuxLoad\_Xfmr}), hanging from the
  same node as the generation.
\end{itemize}

These topologies are depicted in Figs.~\ref{fig:topoS}--\ref{fig:topoM}. If the
producer's topology deviates from these assumptions, the analytic LF calculations will
result in incorrect initialization parameters for the units and therefore the
simulations may start at an unstable state.

\section{Strategy for the calculation}
\label{sec:strategy}

Looking at the above topologies, and knowing that:
\begin{itemize}
\item the magnitudes V, P, Q are always prescribed at the PDR bus;
\item the topology on RTE's side consists of the connection line(s) and, behind them, an
  Infinite Bus in most PCSs. Two variants exist: in PCS I8 the Infinite Bus is replaced
  by an equivalent generator plus equivalent loads, and in the Islanding PCS (I10) there
  is no grid at all --- only loads hanging directly from the PDR bus;
\end{itemize}
a general strategy for the calculation follows:
\begin{enumerate}
\item First choose the angle reference (zero) to be at the PDR bus. Therefore all
  complex magnitudes V, I, S are known at the PDR bus.
\item Start by calculating the complex voltage at the grid bus, behind the connection
  line(s). This is the only initialization value needed on RTE's side (plus the PQ
  injection of the equivalent generator, when one is modeled instead of an Infinite
  Bus). Once this voltage is known, all angles are shifted globally so that \emph{the
    grid bus becomes the angle reference} (this is more convenient for analysts when
  they look at the results). When there is no connection line (the islanding cases), the
  grid bus coincides with the PDR bus and no shift is needed.
\item Then proceed from the PDR bus towards the producer's side, section by section:
  first the internal line (if modeled), then the plant-level transformer (if present),
  then the auxiliary load circuit (if present), and finally the generating unit(s)
  behind their step-up transformers. The calculation of each section uses one of two
  elementary blocks:
  \begin{itemize}
  \item If the section is a line or a transformer with known voltage and flow on one
    side, the calculation simply involves the propagation formulas of a pi-model
    equivalent (Section~\ref{sec:pimodel_lf}).
  \item If the section feeds a constant-PQ load (the auxiliary load), the calculation
    involves a proper two-bus load flow, i.e.\ a second-order algebraic equation, solved
    in closed form (Section~\ref{sec:twobus}).
  \item If there are multiple generating units, the calculation needs one additional
    input from the user: the sharing fractions of their respective PQ injections
    (Section~\ref{sec:gencircuits}).
  \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}

As the calculation progresses, the tool also annotates each element of the network
(line, transformers, load, units) with the initialization values of its terminals
(voltage modulus and angle, P and Q flows, with the signs of each side following the
\Dynawo{} receptor convention); these annotated values are what later gets written into
the case files.




\section{Elementary load-flow blocks}
\label{sec:blocks}

\subsection{Propagation through a pi-model (voltage and flow known at one end)}
\label{sec:pimodel_lf}

All branch sections (connection lines, internal line, and transformers) are handled
through their pi-model equivalent circuit, shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:pimodel_LF}. The
convention is that terminal 1 always represents the bus where both complex voltage and
complex current (or, equivalently, complex voltage and P \& Q) are known, and terminal 2
is where they must be calculated. Appendix~\ref{app:pimodel} shows how to obtain the
pi-model parameters $\Ytr, \Ysh_1, \Ysh_2$ of each kind of branch from the physical
parameters found in the \Dynawo{} case files.

Using current conservation plus the fundamental power equation $S=VI^*$, one has:
\begin{equation*}
  I_{1\rightarrow 2} = \frac{S_1^*}{V_1^*} - V_1 \Ysh_1
                     = ( V_1 - V_2 ) \Ytr
\end{equation*}
Rearranging this equation,
\begin{equation*}
  \frac{S_1^*}{V_1^* \Ytr} - V_1 \frac{\Ysh_1}{\Ytr}
  = V_1 - V_2 \,,
\end{equation*}
we easily obtain the complex voltage at terminal 2:
\begin{equation}
  V_2 = V_1 \left(1 + \frac{\Ysh_1}{\Ytr} \right) - \frac{S_1^*}{V_1^* \Ytr}
  \label{eq:complex_V}
\end{equation}
The current leaving terminal 2 (with the sign conventions indicated by the arrows in
Fig.~\ref{fig:pimodel_LF}) is:
\begin{equation}
  I_2 = ( V_1 - V_2 ) \Ytr - V_2 \Ysh_2 \,
  \label{eq:complex_I}
\end{equation}
so that the complex power flow leaving terminal 2 is:
\begin{equation}
  S_2 \equiv P_2 + j Q_2 = V_2 I_2^*
  \label{eq:complex_S}
\end{equation}

This propagation is implemented in the function \code{\_calc\_pimodel()}. Since it is
used at several steps of the procedure, looking in different directions (towards the
grid, towards the units), one must be careful when handling the signs of its inputs and
outputs: the signs will be correct if one is consistent with the arrows of
Fig.~\ref{fig:pimodel_LF}, as pointed out in the corresponding sections below.

\begin{figure}
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.6\textwidth]{pi_model_circuit}
  \caption{This is the pi-model circuit involved in the basic ``loadflow'' that we solve
    at several steps of the procedure. In our calculations, Terminal 1 always represents
    the bus where both complex voltage and complex current (or, equivalently, complex
    voltage and P \& Q) are known. The complex voltage and current on terminal 2 are
    calculated through simple algebra (so this is not really a loadflow problem,
    strictly speaking). Notation: (tr) stands for \emph{transmission} branch; (sh)
    stands for \emph{shunt} admittance.}
  \label{fig:pimodel_LF}
\end{figure}


\subsection{Transformer terminal orientation}
\label{sec:orientation}

For lines the pi-model is symmetric ($\Ysh_1 = \Ysh_2$, see
Appendix~\ref{app:pimodel}), so it does not matter which physical terminal plays the
role of ``terminal 1'' in Fig.~\ref{fig:pimodel_LF}. For transformers it does matter:
the transformation ratio makes the equivalent pi-model \emph{asymmetric} ($\Ysh_1 \neq
\Ysh_2$), and the parameters given in Appendix~\ref{app:xfmr} are expressed with the
ratio living on the transformer's declared terminal-1 side.

The producer is free to declare his transformers with either orientation (i.e., the
generator may be connected to the transformer's declared terminal 1 or terminal 2).
Therefore, before solving each propagation, the tool checks which declared terminal
faces the bus where magnitudes are known:
\begin{itemize}
\item if the known bus faces the transformer's declared terminal 1, the pi-model is used
  as is;
\item if the known bus faces the declared terminal 2, the same pi circuit must be solved
  as seen from side 2, which simply amounts to \emph{swapping the two shunt admittances}
  ($\Ysh_1 \leftrightarrow \Ysh_2$) in
  equations~\eqref{eq:complex_V}--\eqref{eq:complex_S} (and in the two-bus load flow of
  Section~\ref{sec:twobus}).
\end{itemize}
This check is applied to the plant-level transformer, the auxiliary-load transformer,
and each step-up transformer.


\subsection{The two-bus load flow (voltage known at one end, PQ load at the other)}
\label{sec:twobus}

When the branch feeds a constant-PQ load (the auxiliary load behind its transformer),
the current at terminal 1 is not known in advance, and one must solve a proper two-bus
power-flow problem. It reduces to a quadratic equation, which we solve in closed form.

The circuit is again the pi-model of Fig.~\ref{fig:pimodel_LF}, where now $V_1$ is
known, $S_2$ is known (the load's PQ consumption), and $V_2$ should be solved for. The
equation to solve is:
\begin{equation*}
  (V_1 - V_2) \Ytr = \frac{S_2^*}{V_2^*} + \Ysh_2 V_2 \;.
\end{equation*}
We will solve this in terms of the real and imaginary parts of $V_2$. To prepare for
that, let us rearrange this equation:
\begin{equation*}
  (\Ytr + \Ysh_2) V_2 - \Ytr V_1  +  \frac{S_2^*}{V_2^*}  = 0
\end{equation*}
and now divide the equation by suitable constants:
\begin{equation*}
  \left( \frac{\Ytr + \Ysh_2}{\Ytr} \right) \frac{V_2}{V_1} - 1
  + \left( \frac{S_2^*}{\Ytr \abs{V_1}^2} \right) \frac{V_1^*}{V_2^*}  = 0
\end{equation*}
so that we can redefine the variable and the constants as follows:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:V2defs}
  \begin{split}
  v & \equiv \left( \frac{\Ytr + \Ysh_2}{\Ytr} \right) \frac{V_2}{V_1} \\
  \sigma & \equiv \frac{\left( \Ytr+\Ysh_2 \right)^* S_2^*}{\abs{\Ytr}^2 \, \abs{V_1}^2}
  \end{split}
\end{equation}
in order to simplify the equation:
\begin{equation}
  v - 1 + \frac{\sigma}{v^*}  = 0
\end{equation}

It is now easy to solve this equation in the complex variable $v$ by using the
real and imaginary parts, $v_x + j v_y$. Let us first multiply by $v^*$:
\begin{equation*}
  \abs{v}^2 - v^* + \sigma = 0 \;.
\end{equation*}
The two equations are then:
\begin{align*}
  v_x^2 + v_y^2 - v_x + \sigma_x  & = 0 \\
  v_y + \sigma_y  & = 0
\end{align*}
From this we readily obtain $v_y = -\sigma_y$, and therefore arrive at this
quadratic equation for $v_x$:
\begin{equation*}
  v_x^2 - v_x + \sigma_x  + \sigma_y^2 = 0
\end{equation*}
For which the solution is:
\begin{equation*}
  v_x = \frac{1}{2} \pm \sqrt{\frac{1}{4} - \sigma_x - \sigma_y^2}
\end{equation*}
Here the correct solution branch is obviously the one with the plus sign, as this would
yield a high voltage ($v=1$) if the loads were zero ($\sigma=0$), as expected. Therefore
the complex voltage is:
\begin{equation}
  v = \frac{1}{2} + \sqrt{\frac{1}{4} - \sigma_x - \sigma_y^2} - j \sigma_y
  \label{eq:twobus_v}
\end{equation}
from which one readily obtains $V_2$ by undoing the definitions in
Eq.~\eqref{eq:V2defs}. (If the load were too large to be sustained at the given voltage,
the discriminant in Eq.~\eqref{eq:twobus_v} would become negative and no physical
solution would exist; such a case cannot be initialized.)

Lastly, the net current that flows from terminal 1 into the branch can be
calculated as:
\begin{equation}
  I_1 = (V_1 - V_2) \Ytr + V_1 \Ysh_1
  \label{eq:twobus_I1}
\end{equation}
This two-bus load flow is implemented in the function \code{\_calc\_twobus\_pf()}. The
terminal-orientation rule of Section~\ref{sec:orientation} applies here as well.


\subsection{Zero-impedance connections}
\label{sec:zeroimp}

Some PCSs do not model any connection line on the grid side (notably the islanding
cases, where there is no grid at all). Internally, the missing branch is represented as
a pi-model of \emph{zero impedance}: $\Ytr = \infty$, $\Ysh_1 = \Ysh_2 = 0$. The
propagation formulas~\eqref{eq:complex_V}--\eqref{eq:complex_S} degenerate in that limit
($V_2 \to V_1$, $S_2 \to S_1$), but they cannot be evaluated numerically with an
infinite admittance; the tool therefore detects this case explicitly (function
\code{\_zero\_imp\_line()}) and short-circuits the calculation: the two buses are the
same bus, so voltage and flow carry over unchanged, and no global angle shift is
performed (the PDR bus itself remains the angle reference).




\section{First LF: the grid side (common to all topologies)}
\label{sec:gridside}

The DTR PCSs specify three quantities at the point of connection, the PDR bus: the
voltage modulus and the complex flow $S=P+jQ$. This allows one to propagate the complex
voltage and complex currents on either side of the PDR bus, \emph{independently}. We
start with the grid side because, once the grid-bus voltage is solved for, we can shift
all phases globally so that the grid bus sets the reference (zero angle).

Three remarks about the inputs of this first propagation:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Sign convention.} All flows in the tool follow the \Dynawo{} convention
  (``loads are positive''), so the flow prescribed at the PDR bus, being an injection
  from the producer towards the grid, is held internally as a negative quantity
  $\Spdr$. Looking towards the grid, the flow that enters the connection line is
  therefore $-\Spdr$ (positive).
\item \textbf{Loads at the PDR bus.} Some PCSs (notably the Islanding PCS) model loads
  hanging \emph{directly} from the PDR bus. These loads consume part of the producer's
  delivery before it enters the connection line, and must not be confused with the
  equivalent loads modeled behind the line on the grid side (as in PCS I8). The tool
  separates the two groups by their connection point, and the flow entering the line
  becomes:
  \begin{equation}
    S_1 = -\Spdr - \sum \Saux^{\textrm{(PDR)}}
    \label{eq:pdrloads}
  \end{equation}
  where $\Saux^{\textrm{(PDR)}}$ are the PQ consumptions of the loads connected at the
  PDR bus.
\item \textbf{Multiple parallel lines.} When the grid side models several parallel
  connection lines, they are aggregated into a single equivalent pi-model by adding
  their admittances (see Appendix~\ref{app:parallel}).
\end{itemize}

With $V_1 = \Vpdr$ (angle zero) and $S_1$ as above, the propagation
formulas~\eqref{eq:complex_V}--\eqref{eq:complex_S} directly yield the complex voltage
$V_2 = V_{\textrm{grid}}$ at the grid bus, which is the value used to initialize the
Infinite Bus model. All angles are then shifted globally by $-\arg(V_{\textrm{grid}})$,
so that the grid bus becomes the angle reference and the PDR bus keeps the (now
negative) relative angle. If there is no connection line at all, the zero-impedance
treatment of Section~\ref{sec:zeroimp} applies instead: $V_{\textrm{grid}} = \Vpdr$ and
no shift is performed.

If instead of an infinite bus a real generator is modeled on the grid side (as is the
case of PCS I8), we also need the PQ injection with which to initialize that generator.
Equations~\eqref{eq:complex_I} and \eqref{eq:complex_S} provide the total complex
injection $S_2$ at the grid bus; the generator's initialization values are then obtained
by simply subtracting from $S_2$ the value (with the proper sign) of the equivalent PQ
loads modeled at that bus, if any are present.




\section{Second LF: the producer's internal network}
\label{sec:internal}

We now proceed from the PDR bus towards the producer's side. The known magnitudes are
$\Vpdr$ (with its shifted angle) and $\Spdr$; as anticipated, the sign of the flow is
opposite to the one used on the grid side, since we are now looking in the opposite
direction ($P$ was positive there and is negative here, as per the \Dynawo{} ``loads are
positive'' convention).

Two sections may exist between the PDR bus and the node where the generation hangs
(the ``internal bus''); each of them, when present, is solved with the propagation
formulas~\eqref{eq:complex_V}--\eqref{eq:complex_S}, feeding the output of one section
as the input of the next:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The internal line} (topologies ``+i''): a plain symmetric pi-model
  (Appendix~\ref{app:line}).
\item \textbf{The plant-level transformer} \code{Main\_Xfmr} (topologies of the M
  family): the asymmetric pi-model of Appendix~\ref{app:xfmr}, which accounts for the
  transformation ratio $r$. The terminal-orientation rule of
  Section~\ref{sec:orientation} decides whether the shunts must be swapped, depending on
  which declared terminal of the transformer faces the PDR side.
\end{enumerate}

When neither section is modeled (topologies S and S+Aux), the internal bus is the PDR
bus itself, and the voltage and flow carry over unchanged.




\section{The generating unit circuits}
\label{sec:gencircuits}

At this point the complex voltage $V_{\textrm{int}}$ and the flow $S_{\textrm{int}}$ at
the internal bus are known (when there is an auxiliary load, $S_{\textrm{int}}$ must
first be corrected as described in Section~\ref{sec:auxcircuits}). It remains to
propagate them through each unit's step-up transformer, in order to obtain the voltage
at the unit's terminal and its PQ output.

\subsection{Single unit (topologies S, S+i, S+Aux, S+Aux+i)}

The whole flow $S_{\textrm{int}}$ goes through the single step-up transformer. One thus
obtains the complex voltage at the unit's terminal with
expression~\eqref{eq:complex_V}, and the PQ output of the unit through
expressions~\eqref{eq:complex_I} and \eqref{eq:complex_S} --- always applying the
terminal-orientation rule of Section~\ref{sec:orientation} to the transformer's
pi-model. Then $\Sgen = S_2 = V_2 I_2^*$.

When the step-up transformer is omitted (models with \code{ConverterLVControl = false},
allowed in topology S only), no propagation is needed: the unit's terminal \emph{is}
the internal bus, so it is initialized directly with $V_{\textrm{int}}$ and
$S_{\textrm{int}}$.

\subsection{Multiple units (topologies M, M+i, M+Aux, M+Aux+i)}

When attempting to perform this last propagation with several units, one needs more
data; namely, how the net injection is \emph{shared} among them. The tool obtains these
proportionality factors for P and Q from the user input: the entries
\code{P\_sharing\_<unit\_id>} and \code{Q\_sharing\_<unit\_id>} that the user provides
in the producer's \code{.ini} file, one pair per unit $k$:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{split}
    \lambda_P^{(k)} & = \frac{P_{\textrm{sh}}^{(k)}}{\sum_{i=1}^n P_{\textrm{sh}}^{(i)}} \\
    \lambda_Q^{(k)} & = \frac{Q_{\textrm{sh}}^{(k)}}{\sum_{i=1}^n Q_{\textrm{sh}}^{(i)}}
  \end{split}
  \label{eq:sharing}
\end{equation}
where $n$ is the number of generating units. The tool validates that the fractions of
each magnitude add up to 1 (for a single unit they default to 1, so this reduces
naturally to the previous subsection). These factors are applied separately to the real
and imaginary parts of the net injection at the internal bus:
\begin{equation*}
  S_{\textrm{int}}^{(k)} = \lambda_P^{(k)} P_{\textrm{int}} +
                         j \, \lambda_Q^{(k)} Q_{\textrm{int}}
\end{equation*}
and each share is then propagated through the corresponding unit's step-up transformer
exactly as in the single-unit case, yielding the complex voltage and PQ values at each
unit's terminals.




\section{The auxiliary load circuit (topologies +Aux)}
\label{sec:auxcircuits}

As can be seen in Figs.~\ref{fig:topoS} and \ref{fig:topoM} (right-hand sides), when an
auxiliary load is present the unit output PQ cannot be calculated until we know how much
current goes into the auxiliary load circuit vs.\ the generation circuit. Therefore, in
the ``+Aux'' topologies, an additional load flow must be solved at the internal bus
\emph{before} the generating unit circuits of Section~\ref{sec:gencircuits}.

Since the PQ consumption of the load is specified, and the complex voltage
$V_{\textrm{int}}$ at the HV side of the auxiliary-load transformer is known, this is
exactly the two-bus load flow of Section~\ref{sec:twobus} (with the transformer's
asymmetric pi-model and the terminal-orientation rule of
Section~\ref{sec:orientation}). It yields the voltage at the load terminal --- used to
initialize the load --- and, through Eq.~\eqref{eq:twobus_I1}, the net current
$I_{\textrm{aux}}$ drawn by the auxiliary circuit from the internal bus.

The flow available to the generating units is then obtained by subtracting that current:
\begin{equation}
  S_{\textrm{int}}^{\textrm{(gens)}}
    = V_{\textrm{int}} \left( \frac{S_{\textrm{int}}^*}{V_{\textrm{int}}^*}
      - I_{\textrm{aux}} \right)^{\!*}
\end{equation}
and this corrected $S_{\textrm{int}}^{\textrm{(gens)}}$ is what enters the calculations
of Section~\ref{sec:gencircuits} (the whole of it for a single unit, or split with the
sharing factors of Eq.~\eqref{eq:sharing} for multiple units).

\section{Pi-model equivalent parameters}
\label{app:pimodel}

In this section we describe how to obtain the pi-model parameters $\Ytr, \Ysh_1,
\Ysh_2$ from the physical parameters that we can read from the \Dynawo{} case
files. These conversions are implemented in the functions \code{line\_pimodel()} and
\code{xfmr\_pimodel()} (module \code{dycov/electrical/pimodel\_parameters.py}).


\subsection{Single line}
\label{app:line}

This is quite simple, as \Dynawo's Line model is already in the form of a
pi-model equivalent. Pay attention, though: the shunt admittances are already
divided by two (they follow Eurostag's convention: they are
``half-admittance'').  Therefore one has:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{split}
    \Ytr   & = \frac{1}{\code{line\_RPu} + j \, \code{line\_XPu}} \\
    \Ysh_1 & = \code{line\_GPu} + j \, \code{line\_BPu} \\
    \Ysh_2 & = \code{line\_GPu} + j \, \code{line\_BPu}
  \end{split}
\end{equation}


\subsection{Multiple parallel lines}
\label{app:parallel}

In this case one would simply \emph{add} the admittances, as both the transmission and
the shunt admittances are connected in parallel to each other, respectively.
\begin{equation}
  \begin{split}
    \Ytr   & = \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{1}{\code{line\_RPu}_i + j \, \code{line\_XPu}_i} \\
    \Ysh_1 & = \sum_{i=1}^n \code{line\_GPu}_i + j \, \code{line\_BPu}_i \\
    \Ysh_2 & = \sum_{i=1}^n \code{line\_GPu}_i + j \, \code{line\_BPu}_i
  \end{split}
\end{equation}





\subsection{Transformers}
\label{app:xfmr}

For transformers, one needs to pay attention to the internal modeling used in \Dynawo.
This is the equivalent circuit and conventions:
\begin{quote}
  \begin{verbatim}
               I1  r                I2
    U1,P1,Q1 -->---oo----R+jX-------<-- U2,P2,Q2
  (terminal1)                   |      (terminal2)
                               G+jB
    r=U2/U1                     |
                               ---
  \end{verbatim}
\end{quote}
In the following, we will use the notation $Z \equiv R+jX$ (transmission branch)
and $Y_m \equiv G + jB$ (magnetizing branch).  We will use now this circuit
to obtain the values of its pi-model equivalent $\Ytr, \Ysh_1, \Ysh_2$ in terms of
$Z$ and $Y_m$. Note that, for this calculation, we will be using the convention
that current $I2$ flows in the other direction (see Fig.~\ref{fig:pimodel_LF}),
but this does not affect the final formulas.

Let us first recall the pi-model circuit in Fig.~\ref{fig:pimodel_LF}. It is
straightforward to write the circuit equations:
\begin{equation*}
  \begin{split}
    I_1 & = (V_1 - V_2) \Ytr + V_1 \Ysh_1 \\
    I_2 & = \left(V_1 - V_2\right) \Ytr - V_2 \Ysh_2
  \end{split}
\end{equation*}
We will re-arrange it in this form, which reminds us better of the familiar
matrix equation $I = Y V$:
\begin{equation}
  \label{eq:pimodelIV}
  \begin{split}
    I_1 & = \left( \Ytr + \Ysh_1 \right) V_1 - \Ytr V_2 \\
    I_2 & = \Ytr V_1 - \left( \Ytr + \Ysh_2 \right) V_2
  \end{split}
\end{equation}
Now the aim is to obtain the analogous set of equations for the transformer's
equivalent circuit, so that we can then identify each of these four
coefficients between the two.

The transformer circuit can be solved from first principles as follows. We will
use the following notation:
\begin{itemize}
\item $e_1, i_1$: voltage and current at the ideal section of the transformer,
  on terminal 1 side.
\item $e_2, i_2$: voltage and current at the ideal section of the transformer,
  on terminal 2 side.
\end{itemize}
Looking at the diagram, we readily observe that $e_1=V_1$ and $i_1=I_1$. Now the
two fundamental relations of the transformer are the following:
\begin{align*}
    r & \equiv \frac{e_2}{e_1}  &  & \text{definition of the tap ratio} \\
    e_1 i_1^* & = e_2 i_2^* &  & \text{energy conservation (ideal transformer)} \\
\end{align*}

For $I_1$, we obtain, step by step:
\begin{equation*}
  \begin{split}
    I_1 & = i_1 \\
    & = \frac{e_2^*}{e_1^*} i_2 \\
    & = r i_2 \\
    & = \frac{r}{Z} (e_2 - V_2) \\
    & = \frac{r}{Z} (r e_1 - V_2) \\
    & = \frac{r^2}{Z} V_1 - \frac{r}{Z} V_2 \\
  \end{split}
\end{equation*}
And for $I_2$:
\begin{equation*}
  \begin{split}
    I_2 & = i_2 - V_2 Y_m \\
    & = \frac{1}{Z} (e_2 - V_2) - V_2 Y_m \\
    & = \frac{1}{Z} (r V_1 - V_2) - V_2 Y_m \\
    & = \frac{r}{Z} V_1  - \left( \frac{1}{Z} + Y_m \right) V_2
  \end{split}
\end{equation*}
Equating now the coefficients in these two last equations to those of
Eq.\eqref{eq:pimodelIV}, one finally obtains:
\begin{equation}
  \begin{split}
    \Ytr & = \frac{r}{Z} \\
    \Ysh_1 & = \frac{r(r-1)}{Z} \\
    \Ysh_2 & = \frac{1-r}{Z} + Y_m
  \end{split}
\end{equation}

Note that these parameters are expressed as seen from the transformer's declared
terminal 1 (the ratio $r$ makes them asymmetric, $\Ysh_1 \neq \Ysh_2$). Whenever the bus
with known magnitudes faces the declared terminal 2 instead, the pi-model must be used
with its shunts swapped, as explained in Section~\ref{sec:orientation}.

Every transformer of the topology --- the step-up transformers of the units, the
auxiliary-load transformer, and the plant-level transformer \code{Main\_Xfmr} --- is
converted through these same formulas.
